Most owners I work with have already tried to delegate. They have had the
conversation, named the person, and meant it. Six weeks later the decision is
back on their desk, and the conclusion they draw is about the person: *they
weren't ready*, or *I picked the wrong one*.

It is almost never about the person.

## Three conditions

A decision can only sit with someone else when three things are true at once.
Remove any one of them and the decision travels back up, regardless of who you
handed it to.

1. **They know what "good" looks like.** Not the process — the outcome. If the
   only way to know whether a decision was right is to ask you, then you are
   still making it, just more slowly.
2. **They can see the information you see.** Delegating a pricing decision to
   someone who cannot see margin data is not delegation. It is a request for a
   guess that you will then have to correct.
3. **The cost of being wrong is survivable, and everyone knows it.** People
   escalate when they cannot tell whether a mistake is recoverable. Say out
   loud which decisions are reversible and you will stop receiving them.

## The test

Take one decision that came back to you this week and check it against the
three. In my experience the second condition fails most often, and it is also
the cheapest to fix — usually it is a report someone does not have access to
rather than a capability they lack.

## What this is not

This is not an argument for letting go of everything. Some decisions genuinely
should sit with you: the ones that are expensive, irreversible, and rare. The
problem is not that you make decisions. It is that the boundary has never been
written down, so every decision is potentially yours, and your team behaves
accordingly.

Write the boundary down. That single act removes more escalations than any
amount of encouragement to "take ownership."
